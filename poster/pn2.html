Background and Motivation

In a conventional transceiver design, coding, modulation, channel estimation and detection are designed as separate blocks. Each block is optimal for the job it is given, but the receiver as a whole is only as good as the assumptions that connect them. Deep learning offers two alternatives: train transmitter and receiver together as an autoencoder [1], or replace channel estimation and detection with a learned receiver [2].

We ask two questions:

  • How much is end-to-end learning worth compared with a classical receiver that is already doing its best?
  • Can the transmitter be trained at all when no differentiable model of the channel is available?

Objectives and Contributions

  • Measure the gain of a learned transceiver against a matched classical baseline, on AWGN and on Rayleigh block fading with pilot-estimated CSI.
  • Train the transmitter with no channel model, using reinforcement learning instead of backpropagation through the channel, and measure the cost.
  • Account for the pilot overhead and the channel estimation error. Let R be the code rate, L the number of symbols in one fading block, P the number of pilot symbols in that block, N0 the noise power spectral density and ĥ the estimated channel coefficient. The effective code rate is then R(L−P)/L, and after zero-forcing the effective noise is N0(1+1/P)/|ĥ|².
  • Identify the cause of each failure by experiment: keep the weights fixed, change one thing at a time, and see which change removes the symptom.

Where the AWGN Gain Comes From

Under AWGN, APP demapping is already optimal, so the improvement cannot come from the receiver. It comes from the transmitter: the learned constellation moves away from the regular 64-QAM lattice and gives more power to the symbols that the demapper confuses most often.
